1 · The honest reality of going "direct"

You asked to replace Beds24 entirely and control every channel directly. That is the right long-term ambition — it maximises control, removes a dependency, and unlocks each OTA's full feature set — but it's important to be clear-eyed about what it costs, because that cost shapes the whole build.

Beds24 exists precisely because direct connectivity is hard. Every major OTA gates direct access behind a partner contract and a certification process. Nobody hands you a public "push my rooms" API you can sign up for on a Tuesday.

What "direct" really requires, per OTA
  • A signed connectivity-partner contract with each OTA.
  • Certification — passing their test suite for message accuracy, before you can push live inventory (weeks to months each).
  • Ongoing maintenance — OTAs deprecate API versions and change schemas continually; a live connection is a permanent engineering commitment.
  • Airbnb has no public API at all. It is invite-only; Airbnb chooses its software partners and routinely turns down smaller vendors. A direct Airbnb connection may simply not be grantable in the near term.
The pragmatic engineering answer: adapter-per-OTA

Build the system direct-first and Beds24-free by design, but make every channel a pluggable connector behind one interface. Then you can certify and switch on Booking.com, Expedia and Agoda directly as each certification lands — and for anything you can't self-certify yet (Airbnb above all), drop in a white-label connectivity API such as Channex.io as one more connector, with no change to the core. Beds24 is gone; the architecture that replaces it is strictly more capable.

Leading channel managers maintain 450+ certified OTA connections (SiteMinder, Cloudbeds) — a number no single property or small vendor can replicate. The realistic target isn't 450; it's direct on the handful that drive your volume, with a fallback connector for the long tail. The connector architecture below makes both true at once.

2 · What your prototype is today

Helio (the PMS-UI-Testing app) is a genuinely impressive high-fidelity front-end prototype — React 19 + Vite, Dexie/IndexedDB for local state, TanStack Query with a mock fetcher, i18n, PWA. The Channel Manager screen alone is ~2,000 lines across 18 tabs (Distribution, Auto-feed, Connection, Mappings, Inventory map, Sync schedule, Channels, Content, Policies, Push & Pull, Inbox, Conflicts, Drift, Sync log, Errors, Parity, Audit, Reports). The domain modelling is excellent.

3 · Target architecture

One master calendar in the PMS. One engine that fans changes out to many direct connectors. One inbound loop that pulls every booking back and re-balances inventory everywhere. No aggregator in the path.

*Airbnb and any un-certifiable long-tail channel ride a white-label connector (e.g. Channex.io) — the same interface, so the core never knows the difference.

The five load-bearing ideas

1. PMS is the single source of truth

Rates, availability and restrictions live in one master calendar. Connectors are pure distributors of what it says — they never hold their own truth. This is the property every serious system shares (SiteMinder, Cloudbeds, D-EDGE).

2. Pooled inventory, not per-channel allotments

Every channel draws from the same availability count. Sell a room anywhere and it decrements everywhere in real time — maximum exposure, overbooking-safe. (The old "give Booking 2, give Expedia 2" model strands inventory.)

3. Derived / linked rates off one BAR parent

Non-refundable = BAR −10%; B&B = BAR +$15; single-occ = BAR −20%. Children recalculate automatically when BAR changes, so one edit stays parity-clean across every channel. No manual per-channel repricing.

4. Connector = adapter-per-OTA

A single Connector interface: pushAri(), testConnection(), normalizeBooking(), plus capabilities + certification state. Each OTA's protocol (OTA-XML / REST / GraphQL) lives inside its adapter; the engine only speaks the interface.

5. Reservation-delivery loop = the overbooking guard

When any OTA delivers a booking, its adapter normalizes it, the source of truth decrements the shared pool for the stay window, and the engine re-pushes the affected dates to every other live channel. That closing loop — not the outbound push — is what actually prevents cross-channel overbooking once Beds24's own guard is gone.

Bed-level (hostel) inventory is a first-class case

Your system already models dorms and beds — good, because hostels are where generic tools fall over. Hostelworld sells per bed: occupancy = beds, so a 3-bed dorm at $15 pushes three $15 bed listings, and a private double is sent per-person. The Hostelworld connector carries a bedLevel: true capability and expands pooled dorm inventory into bed units on push. Only a handful of engines do this (Cloudbeds, Beds24, Sirvoy) — keeping it native is a real differentiator.

6 · Phased roadmap

Sequenced so value lands early and nothing depends on a certification you don't yet hold. Beds24 is decommissioned by design, but the connector abstraction means you're never blocked waiting on one OTA.

1
Re-model the domain (done in the engine; mirror into the PMS UI)
Replace beds24RoomMappings / beds24BedId / "PMS vs Beds24 drift" with the channel-agnostic Connector model. Each OTA becomes a first-class connection with protocol, credentials, capabilities and certification state. The Channel Manager screen's "Connection" tab goes from one Beds24 card to a grid of direct connections; "Auto-feed from Beds24" becomes "map your PMS inventory to each OTA."
2
Stand up the engine as the PMS's distribution service
Point the PMS at helio-channel-engine's REST API (already running). Wire Rates & Inventory screens to POST /ari; wire the OTA inbox to /webhooks/:channel. At this stage everything works against mock connectors — full product, no live OTA yet.
3
Certify Booking.com first (highest volume, most accessible tier)
Sign the Connectivity Partner contract, get machine-account credentials, fill in adapters/bookingcom.ts's transport, pass certification, flip certification: 'certified'. The engine starts pushing live to Booking.com the moment the flag flips — nothing else changes.
4
Certify Expedia (+ Vrbo) and Agoda in parallel
Same pattern per adapter. Add the DIRECT booking-engine connector (0% commission, always in-parity) so your own website is a peer channel.
5
Cover the gaps with a white-label connector
Add a ChannexConnector for Airbnb (no direct API) and the long-tail regional OTAs. Certify Hostelworld directly for bed-level control. Turn on the parity monitor, drift alerts, and (later) yield rules / AI repricing.
Result: Beds24 fully removed; Booking.com, Expedia/Vrbo, Agoda and Hostelworld running as owned direct connections; Airbnb + long tail served through one white-label connector — all behind a single engine you control, with the PMS as source of truth.

7 · Recommendation & risks

You chose "replace Beds24 entirely." The architecture here honours that — Beds24 is gone from the design — while protecting you from its one real hazard: being unable to reach a channel you don't control. Two honest caveats:

  • Don't double-connect a property to the same OTA through two paths. If any channel is ever served both directly and through a fallback connector, you get rate/availability conflicts and overbookings. The clean rule: exactly one connector owns each channel.
  • Certification is calendar time you can't compress with code. Budget months, not weeks, before all priority channels are live-direct. The mock connectors let you ship and demo the complete product in the meantime.

Bottom line: the connector-per-OTA engine is the correct foundation whether you end up 100% direct or direct-plus-fallback. It's built and running. The next concrete build step is mirroring its data model into the front-end and wiring the Channel Manager screens to it — turning the Beds24 hub into the direct-connections control surface.
